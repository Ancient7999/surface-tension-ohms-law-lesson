Lab 8

Surface tension of a liquid

Aim

To determine the surface tension of a liquid by the capillary rise method.

Intended learning outcomes

  • Determine the surface tension of a liquid by the capillary rise method.
  • Explain the factors on which surface tension depends.
  • Represent the data with accuracy.

Medical perspectives

Lung function and alveolar stability

The alveoli are tiny, curved, liquid-lined air sacs. The surface tension of that lining pulls each one inward, so a pressure difference ΔP = 2γ/r is needed to keep it open.

Surfactant prevents collapse

Type II alveolar cells secrete pulmonary surfactant. It lowers the surface tension of the lining, most of all in small alveoli, so small and large alveoli stay open together and breathing takes less work.

Respiratory disorders

Premature babies may not yet make enough surfactant, causing neonatal respiratory distress syndrome; replacement surfactant is given into the airway. Damaged surfactant also stiffens the lungs in acute respiratory distress syndrome.

Lab 8 · Theory

Why a liquid climbs a narrow tube

A liquid surface behaves like a stretched skin. Where that skin meets glass, it pulls the liquid up the wall; in a narrow bore the pull can lift a column several centimetres high.

Cohesion, adhesion and surface tension

Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same liquid. A molecule deep inside is pulled equally in every direction, so the pulls cancel. A molecule at the surface has neighbours only below and beside it, so it feels a net pull inward.

The surface therefore tends to shrink to the smallest possible area, as if it were an elastic film. Surface tension γ is the force per unit length acting along the surface: γ = F / L.

Adhesion is the attraction between the liquid and another material, such as glass. Whether a liquid wets a surface depends on adhesion compared with cohesion.

Units: dyne/cm in the CGS system. 1 dyne/cm = 10−3 N/m, so water's 72 dyne/cm is 0.072 N/m.

Contact angle

The contact angle θ is measured inside the liquid, between the glass wall and the liquid surface where they meet.

Capillary rise: where the formula comes from

  1. The surface pulls along the whole inner rim of the bore, a length 2πr. Its vertical part is γ cos θ per unit length.upward force = 2π r γ cos θ
  2. The raised column is a cylinder of radius r and height h. Its weight is its volume × density × g.weight = π r² h ρ g
  3. The liquid stops rising when the two balance.2π r γ cos θ = π r² h ρ g
  4. Dividing both sides by πr and solving for γ:γ = h r ρ g ÷ (2 cos θ)
  5. For water in clean glass θ = 0°, cos 0° = 1, so the formula reduces to γ = h r ρ g ÷ 2

What surface tension depends on

Nature of the liquid

Stronger cohesion means higher γ: mercury about 485, water 72, ethanol 22 dyne/cm.

Temperature

γ falls as temperature rises, because the molecules move faster and attract less effectively. Water: about 75.6 dyne/cm at 0 °C, 72.0 at 25 °C, 58.9 at 100 °C.

Impurities

Dissolved substances change γ. Grease or oil on the glass also changes the contact angle, which is why the capillary is cleaned first.

Surfactants

Soap and detergent molecules gather at the surface and lower γ strongly, to about 25–35 dyne/cm for a soap solution.

Laplace law and the alveoli

A curved liquid surface pushes on the side it curves toward. For a spherical surface of radius r, the pressure inside is higher than outside by

ΔP = 2γ / r

A soap bubble has two surfaces (inside and outside of the film), so for it ΔP = 4γ / r.

An alveolus of radius 0.01 cm lined with a watery film of γ = 72 dyne/cm would need ΔP = 2 × 72 ÷ 0.01 = 14 400 dyne/cm², about 14.7 cm H₂O, just to stay open. With surfactant bringing γ down to about 25 dyne/cm, the same alveolus needs only about 5 cm H₂O.

Why surfactant matters

By ΔP = 2γ/r, a small alveolus with the same γ would have a higher pressure than a large one and would empty into it. Surfactant molecules crowd together as an alveolus shrinks, lowering γ most in the smallest alveoli. The pressures even out and the lung stays evenly inflated.

Steps

Good practice
  • Keep the capillary exactly vertical; a tilted tube gives a longer, wrong column.
  • Read at eye level with the meniscus to avoid parallax, at the bottom of the curve.
  • Use the radius, not the diameter, and keep every length in cm.
  • Wet the inside of the tube before each reading so the meniscus forms the same way every time.
Care
  • Capillary tubes are thin glass and break easily; handle them gently and dispose of broken glass safely.
  • Keep ethanol away from flames.

Using d = 0.10 cm gives γ twice too large (144 dyne/cm). The formula needs r = d ÷ 2.

    Lab 9

    Ohm's law and an unknown resistance

    Aim

    To verify Ohm's law and determine the unknown resistance.

    Intended learning outcomes

    • Verify Ohm's law and find the unknown resistance.
    • Plot the graph between current and voltage, and analyse the experimental error.

    Medical perspectives

    Electrical resistance of body tissues

    Blood, muscle and other watery tissues conduct well; fat, bone and dry skin conduct poorly. Dry skin can be around 100 kΩ, wet skin only about 1 kΩ, and the body's interior a few hundred ohms.

    ECG and bioimpedance

    An ECG measures tiny voltages on the skin, so electrodes with gel keep the contact resistance low. Bioimpedance devices pass a small, safe current and use V = IR to estimate body water and fat.

    Electrical safety

    The current through a body is I = V/R, so low skin resistance is dangerous. Around 1 mA is felt, about 10 mA can stop a person letting go, and around 100 mA across the chest can cause ventricular fibrillation.

    Lab 9 · Theory

    Current, voltage and resistance

    At a fixed temperature, the current through a metallic conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it. The constant of proportionality is set by the resistance.

    Resistance and Ohm's law

    V = I R⇔I = (1/R) V⇒slope of I against V = 1/R
    V · volt

    potential difference across the component: energy per coulomb of charge passing through

    I · ampere

    current: the rate of flow of charge, 1 A = 1 C/s

    R · ohm (Ω)

    resistance, 1 Ω = 1 V/A; for a wire R = ρL/A, so it grows with length and falls with cross-section

    With I on the y-axis and V on the x-axis, the graph of an ohmic resistor is a straight line through the origin. A steeper line means a smaller resistance.

    Ohmic and non-ohmic components

    Internal resistance of the supply

    A real supply behaves like an ideal EMF E in series with a small internal resistance r. When a current I flows, part of the EMF is used inside the supply:

    V = E − I r

    So the voltmeter across the resistor reads a little less than the supply setting, and the gap grows with the current. This does not spoil the experiment: R is found from the V and I actually measured across the resistor.

    Connecting the meters

    Ammeter: in series

    The same current must pass through the ammeter and the resistor, so the ammeter is placed in the loop. Its resistance is very small, so it hardly changes the current. Connected across the resistor (in parallel) it short-circuits it and a large current flows: this can damage the meter.

    Voltmeter: in parallel

    The voltmeter measures the potential difference between the two ends of the resistor, so it is connected across them. Its resistance is very large, so it takes almost no current. Placed in series, it blocks the current and the ammeter reads almost zero.

    Polarity matters for moving-coil meters: current enters at the + (red) terminal. Connected the wrong way round, the needle tries to move below zero.

    Body tissue impedance and safety

    The body is a conductor made of salty fluids divided by cell membranes. For direct current the membranes act like insulators, while alternating current can pass through them, so the body's opposition to current, its impedance, depends on frequency. Bioimpedance analysis uses this to estimate body water and fat-free mass.

    Skin carries most of the resistance. Ohm's law shows why wet skin is dangerous: with 230 V across about 1000 Ω, I = 230 ÷ 1000 = 0.23 A, more than enough to stop the heart. With 100 000 Ω of dry skin the same voltage drives about 2.3 mA.

    Medical equipment is therefore earthed, insulated and fitted with current limits, and devices touching the heart directly have far stricter leakage-current limits than those on the skin.

    Current through the body

    ≈ 1 mA: just felt
    ≈ 10 mA: muscles clamp, hard to let go
    ≈ 100 mA across the chest: ventricular fibrillation
    Values for mains frequency, hand to hand, about one second.

    Slope upside down

    With I on the y-axis the slope is ΔI/ΔV ≈ 0.01 A/V. ΔV/ΔI ≈ 100 is already R, so taking 1 ÷ it gives 0.01 Ω.
